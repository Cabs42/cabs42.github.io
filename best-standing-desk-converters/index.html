What a standing desk converter actually does

A standing desk converter is a platform that sits on top of a regular desk and raises or lowers to let you work standing up, then drops back down for sitting. It's the cheaper, no-installation alternative to a full height-adjustable desk — useful for renters, anyone who doesn't want to replace existing furniture, or anyone testing whether they'll actually use a standing setup before committing to a bigger purchase.

Manual vs. electric lift

  • Manual (spring or crank) converters use a counterbalanced spring or a hand crank to raise and lower the platform. They're less expensive and have fewer parts to fail, but adjusting height mid-task takes a bit more effort than pressing a button.
  • Electric converters raise and lower at the push of a button, often with memory presets for a sit and a stand height. They cost more and sit taller when fully retracted, but make switching positions effortless enough that people actually do it throughout the day.

What to check before buying

  • Weight capacity vs. your actual setup — add up your monitor(s), keyboard, laptop, and any monitor arm mounted to the platform, and leave margin below the converter's rated limit.
  • Work surface depth — a deeper platform (12+ inches) fits a keyboard and mouse comfortably in front of a monitor; shallow platforms can feel cramped once a keyboard tray is added.
  • Footprint on your existing desk — measure your desk's width and depth before ordering; a converter that's too wide will overhang the edges.
  • Height range at both ends — check the lowest sitting height (should sit close to a normal desk height) and the highest standing height against your own height, not just an average.
  • Keyboard tray tier — a separate lower tier for the keyboard keeps typing height comfortable and stops the monitor from sitting too high relative to your arms.

Practical desk tips

When standing, aim for elbows bent around 90 degrees with the keyboard at that height and the monitor top at eye level — the same targets as a sitting setup, just raised. Start with shorter standing intervals and build up; most people don't stand all day, and alternating through the day is the realistic, sustainable pattern.

Bottom line

For most home offices, a converter with a deep work surface, a separate keyboard tier, and a weight capacity comfortably above your actual gear is the right pick — choose manual to save money or electric if you want switching positions to be truly effortless.
