Why an ergonomic keyboard

A standard flat keyboard pushes your hands together in front of your body, so your wrists bend outward and your forearms rotate flat. Over long typing days that position is uncomfortable for many people. Ergonomic keyboards change the shape of the board so your hands sit closer to shoulder width and in a more neutral angle. They are a comfort tool, not a medical treatment; if you already have pain, talk to a clinician as well.

The main types

  • Curved one-piece boards — the keys are split into two angled halves inside a single case, usually with a built-in palm rest. Logitech's Ergo K860 is the best-known current example, and it is widely suggested as a replacement for the discontinued Microsoft Sculpt and Natural ergonomic keyboards. The learning curve is short because the layout is otherwise familiar.
  • Fully split boards — two separate halves that you can place as far apart as your shoulders. The Kinesis Freestyle2 is a long-running example. Many split boards support tenting, which raises the inner edges so your palms tilt toward each other.
  • Contoured and columnar boards — keys sit in sculpted wells and in straight columns rather than staggered rows. These offer the most adjustment but take the longest to learn, often a couple of weeks before your typing speed recovers.

What to check before buying

  • How much change you want — a curved board is an easy first step; a fully split or contoured board is a bigger commitment.
  • Wireless vs wired — many ergonomic boards connect over Bluetooth or a USB receiver and can switch between computers. Wired boards avoid batteries and pairing.
  • Palm rest and negative tilt — some boards tilt away from you (negative tilt) or include feet that lower the front edge, which keeps wrists straighter.
  • Number pad — full-size ergonomic boards include one; many split boards drop it to bring your mouse closer.
  • Mac or Windows layout — check that the modifier keys match your computer, or that the board supports remapping.
  • Switch type — membrane and scissor keys are quieter; mechanical switches are more tactile and durable but louder on calls.

Setup tips

Keep the keyboard low enough that your elbows rest at about 90 degrees and your shoulders stay relaxed. Pair it with a mouse that keeps your forearm in a similar neutral position, such as a vertical mouse.

Bottom line

If you just want more comfort without relearning how to type, start with a curved one-piece board. Move to a fully split or tented design if you want maximum adjustability and are willing to put up with a learning curve.
